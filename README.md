## Overview

'Stick Indian' is an English-language cover term for a class of dangerous forest beings appearing in the oral traditions of many Pacific Northwest Indigenous peoples — including the Salish-speaking Coast Salish and Puget Sound peoples, the Yakama, Klickitat, Cayuse, Nez Perce, Wenatchi, Colville, and others. The original Indigenous names (e.g. tsiatko, steta'l, seeahtik) refer to entities that vary substantially across nations: Salish traditions often describe them as tall, hairy, bigfoot-like giants who live in the deep forest, whereas Cayuse and Yakama traditions describe them more often as forest dwarves or 'little people'. Across traditions, the Stick Indians are characterised by whistling, mimicry of human voices, and the ability to lead travellers astray, paralyse, hypnotise, or in some accounts kidnap and even devour them. They are commonly invoked in moral instruction — never whistle at night in the woods, never answer a voice that calls your name from the trees, never speak ill of the forest. Many Pacific Northwest people regard 'Stick Indian' as an English euphemism, holding that pronouncing the original Indigenous name aloud risks attracting their attention; this aspect of the tradition remains active today.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Pacific Northwest Indigenous oral traditions; the English-language phrase 'Stick Indian' is a calque on a Plateau-region usage in which 'stick' translates a word meaning 'forest' or 'woods', so 'Stick Indians' literally means 'Indians of the woods'.
- **Meaning**: 'Stick' in regional Chinook Jargon and Plateau English means 'wood' or 'forest'; the term names beings of the woods. Indigenous names (tsiatko, seeahtik, steta'l) carry their own etymologies in the relevant languages.
- **First use**: 19th-century Plateau English usage; recorded in early-20th-century ethnographies of Pacific Northwest peoples

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Forest spirits / non-human persons** _(Coast Salish elders and storytellers, Yakama, Klickitat, Cayuse, Nez Perce traditions)_

Indigenous traditions hold the beings to be real persons of the forest, distinct from humans, with their own social order and moral expectations of human behaviour.

### Skeptical

**Cultural pedagogy and natural mimicry** _(Academic folklorists)_

From an outside-academic vantage, Stick Indian traditions function in part as moral instruction (do not wander alone, do not disrespect the forest) and explanations for genuinely strange forest sounds — wind in trees, owl calls, marten and weasel chatter — that imitate human whistles or voices.


## Indigenous Perspectives

> ⚠️ **'Stick Indian' is itself an English euphemism: in many traditions, naming the beings in their own language is considered to risk attracting their attention. Outside writers should not assume entitlement to translate or print Indigenous names without community consent.**

**Cultures**: Coast Salish (Lushootseed-speaking peoples), Lummi, Puget Sound Salish peoples, Yakama, Klickitat, Cayuse, Nez Perce, Wenatchi, Colville, Spokane, Klallam, Chehalis

Integral to Pacific Northwest knowledge systems about the forest and proper conduct in the wilderness. Stick Indian traditions are a living part of these cultures and are pedagogically transmitted to children.
